# The Cognitive Transformation Registry

An archive holds fragile research notebooks. Readers want to study them; the archive wants them to remain available to future readers. Opening the originals can expose them to damage. Restricting handling can prevent useful study. Both concerns are intelligible, yet alternating between them may leave the same open-or-closed choice in place.

The earlier prisms made a situation's entities, processes, conditions and bindings explicit. The [circle and the tilt](09_THE_CIRCLE_AND_THE_TILT.md) followed possible changes of view while preserving the shared question. We can now make the choice of movement explicit. The **Cognitive Transformation Registry**, or **CTR**, gives names and conditions to possible movements: change the view, reverse a presumption, separate a compressed label, introduce a missing distinction, or alter a relationship.

The registry helps select a useful movement and state what it must preserve. The next chapter's Exploration and Transform Engine develops the arrangements that movement makes possible.

## 1. Identify what the opening frame leaves unresolved

“Improve access” gives a direction without specifying the difficulty. Does a reader need to consult the words, inspect the paper, compare ink, or examine how pages were bound? The same notebook can support different inquiries. A policy that serves one may fail another.

Likewise, “protect the collection” can conceal different mechanisms. A prohibition on handling, a reproduction service and supervised consultation change access in different ways. Before choosing a transformation, we need enough of the situation to identify which distinction could matter.

*Decomposition* opens the broad labels into the work they summarize. *Comparison* separates the demands of textual study from those of studying the physical object. *Salience reweighting* then makes that difference prominent. Their combination gives a more useful starting point than another argument about whether access or protection matters more.

CTR directs a selected transform toward a particular part of the account. A change of research question concerns the view; replacing handling with an adequate reproduction concerns the process; changing when consultation is allowed concerns its conditions. These changes can cooperate, but naming the target helps us see what each contributes.

## 2. Rotate the view, then reverse a working presumption

From the reader's position, a closed notebook can be unavailable evidence. From the archive's position, repeated handling can consume the collection's future usefulness. A **rotation** keeps the notebooks and both concerns in view while changing which relationship receives attention. It can reveal that preservation itself supports future access, rather than standing wholly outside the reader's purpose.

We can then reverse the working presumption. Suppose handling begins as the normal route to study. What becomes impossible if that route is withheld? Conversely, if handling is generally available, what loss could make later study impossible? This **inversion** exposes consequences hidden by whichever presumption originally seemed natural.

The moves have different jobs. Rotation changes the view of the shared situation. Inversion reverses a direction or burden within it. Both preserve the two concerns while changing what is examined.

The contributing operations are *perspective change*, *comparison* and *counterfactual reasoning*. We hold the two requirements steady and examine the consequences of each presumption. This adds a contrast that can guide further inquiry. If both presumptions leave a consequential failure, merely choosing the other side will not supply a satisfactory arrangement.

## 3. Add the distinction the original choice could not express

The opening question treated access to a notebook's contents and access to the notebook itself as one thing. Separating them changes the available choices. A reproduction might support a question about wording while failing a question about paper or binding. Original handling might therefore be necessary for one request and unnecessary for another.

CTR calls the addition of a missing dimension a **dimensional lift**. The two concerns survive, but the account can now distinguish kinds of access. We have more to work with than one line between fully open and fully closed.

This combines *label decomposition* with *dimensionality change*. *Classification* relates a particular research question to the kind of evidence it needs; *concretization* asks whether a proposed copy actually supplies that evidence. Their combination makes an alternative visible without declaring it sufficient in advance.

The prism analogy is useful here. Walking around an object changes the view; a tilt can expose a relation the earlier plane concealed. In the archive, the additional relation is between a research need and the evidence carrier that can meet it. The distinction earns its place if it changes an access arrangement or reveals why an arrangement fails.

It does not settle every conflict. If the required evidence exists only in the original and the notebook cannot be handled acceptably, the original tension remains. The lift has located that tension more precisely.

## 4. Preserve the purpose while changing the means

Once kinds of access are separate, the archive can consider changing how a restriction works. A general handling limit might become a distinction between routine textual requests and requests that require the original. In the latter case, consultation could remain conditional on the notebook's condition and the availability of supervision.

The purpose of the restriction remains preservation. Its form changes. The registry describes this kind of move as **constraint substitution**: replace a mechanism while retaining the purpose it serves. Whether the replacement serves that purpose is one of the checks the plan must carry.

*Abstraction* isolates the conservation purpose from the present rule. *Recombination* joins different forms of access to different needs. *Evaluation* returns each proposal to meaningful study and continued preservation. Together these moves let an inquiry change the mechanism without quietly discarding its reason for existing.

CTR calls this connection back a **return path**. A proposal to supply reproductions must return to what readers need to examine. A proposal for supervised consultation must return to handling conditions and available capacity. A plan that simply assumes excellent copies and unlimited staff has changed the problem's resources as well as its arrangement.

The minimum useful sequence here may be short: distinguish access needs, introduce their evidence carriers, then examine a different restriction mechanism. The full registry includes many more families. An additional transform is useful when it changes a candidate, an unresolved question, or a consequential check.

## 5. Make the selected movement available for exploration

A compact transform plan can preserve the work without reproducing the whole registry:

| Selected movement | What stays in view | What changes or needs checking |
|---|---|---|
| Rotate the view | Meaningful study and preservation. | Which consequence is prominent. |
| Separate kinds of access | Both concerns and the research question. | Whether content access and object handling can follow different routes. |
| Substitute a restriction mechanism | The preservation purpose. | Whether differentiated access can serve that purpose under actual conditions. |

The result is a plan for developing possibilities. It identifies the movement, its target, the relation it preserves, and the check needed on return. **ETE** can then construct candidate arrangements; **PSPP** compares what those arrangements support. CTR supplies the vocabulary and selection contract for this work.

*Decomposition* located the opening difficulty, *comparison* distinguished useful movements, and *sequencing* related their contributions. Together they provide a deliberate route from the present framing to a different arrangement while keeping the original purpose available for inspection.

A capable model may make these moves through a brief prompt. Supplying the registry makes their choice and boundaries inspectable, which is useful when an application needs to repeat or compare the method. The distinction between knowing a transform's name and applying it remains visible in the resulting arrangement.

A bounded request can begin with the archive's unresolved question:

> An archive holds fragile notebooks. Preserve meaningful study and the collection's future usefulness. Select a small CTR transform sequence that examines the difference between access to contents and handling the original. Explain what each movement preserves and changes. Include a research question for which a copy may be inadequate, retain staffing and copy quality as unknown where unspecified, and state the return checks an explored arrangement would need.
